Galton Board Simulator

Drop beads through a peg board and watch random left-right choices build a distribution. Compare the experimental histogram with the theoretical binomial curve.

Maths Probability Binomial distribution Normal approximation

Learning Intention

Students will understand how repeated random choices can form a predictable distribution over many trials.

Success Criteria

Students can describe how the histogram changes as more beads are dropped and compare experimental results with a theoretical binomial model.

Model note

Each peg decision is modelled as a random left-right choice. With many beads and p = 0.50, the binomial distribution begins to resemble a normal curve.

Student challenge

  • Drop 50 beads. Describe the shape of the histogram.
  • Reset and drop 1000 beads. How does the shape change?
  • Change p(right) to 0.60. What happens to the centre of the distribution?
  • Increase the number of rows. What happens to the number of possible final bins?
  • Export the CSV and compare experimental relative frequency with theoretical probability.

Teacher notes

The final bin number represents how many times a bead moved right. For n rows, the possible final bins are 0 to n. This connects directly to the binomial distribution.

  • Good prompt: Why does the distribution become smoother with more beads?
  • Good prompt: Why does a biased p(right) shift the histogram left or right?
  • Extension: Connect the model to Pascal’s triangle and binomial coefficients.
