What Van Helmont measured

He compared the mass of the willow and the dry soil before and after the growing period.

What he concluded

Because the soil mass changed very little, he argued that the new plant material came from water.

What was missing

Carbon dioxide entering from the air, oxygen leaving, leaf fall and stored water were not properly measured.

Fresh mass and dry biomass

The measured tree mass includes water. Dry biomass is built mostly from atoms from carbon dioxide and water.

Student challenge

  1. Run the historical setup. Record the change in tree mass and soil mass.
  2. Explain why Van Helmont’s conclusion seemed reasonable using only the evidence he measured.
  3. Change sunlight, water and carbon dioxide one at a time. Which variable has the biggest effect in this model?
  4. Write a stronger conclusion that uses modern knowledge of photosynthesis.
  5. Design one improvement to the original experiment that would help measure the role of air.

Teacher notes

This sim is useful for teaching controlled variables, mass balance, validity, historical science and the development of photosynthesis ideas. It deliberately separates Van Helmont’s measured evidence from the modern interpretation, so students can practise evaluating a conclusion rather than simply labelling it wrong.

Historical reference points: Van Helmont’s commonly reported setup used a willow of about 2.3 kg planted in about 91 kg of dried soil for five years. The tree gained about 74.5 kg while the redried soil lost only about 57 g. Source: Biology LibreTexts, Photosynthesis: Discovering the Secrets.
