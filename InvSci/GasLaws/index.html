Gas Laws Technology Simulator

Use a virtual syringe, pressure sensor and temperature control to investigate Boyle’s Law, Charles’ Law and the Combined Gas Law.

Learning Intention

Students will understand that gas pressure, volume and temperature are related, and that sensors and graphs can be used to model gas behaviour.

Success Criteria

Students can manipulate one variable, control or calculate another variable, record evidence, graph a relationship, and evaluate the limitations of a gas law model.

Boyle’s Law

Evidence and model

Current relationship

At constant temperature, pressure and volume have an inverse relationship. As volume decreases, pressure increases.

Syringe question: Why does the pressure rise when the plunger is pushed in?

Investigation steps

  1. Keep temperature constant.
  2. Change volume.
  3. Record pressure.
  4. Describe the graph pattern.

Student challenge

  1. Use Boyle’s Law mode. Record at least six trials and describe the relationship between pressure and volume.
  2. Use Charles’ Law mode. Record at least six trials and explain why temperature must be measured in kelvin for gas law calculations.
  3. Use Combined Gas Law mode. Choose one variable to calculate and test whether PV/T stays approximately constant.
  4. Change the sensor uncertainty. Explain how measurement uncertainty affects the quality of the data.
  5. Choose one application context and explain how the model connects to a real technology.

CER response

Teacher notes

  • Primary module: Module 6, Technologies. Cross-module links: Module 5 and Module 7.
  • The model assumes a fixed amount of gas and simplified ideal gas behaviour.
  • Boyle’s Law mode calculates pressure from changing volume at constant temperature.
  • Charles’ Law mode calculates volume from changing temperature at constant pressure.
  • Combined Gas Law mode lets students calculate pressure, volume or temperature while manipulating the other two variables.
  • Students should assess the simulation by identifying strengths, limitations, differences from a real investigation, possible measurement error, and an overall usefulness judgement.
  • Source base: OpenStax Chemistry 2e, NASA Glenn Research Center and Chemistry LibreTexts gas laws resources.
