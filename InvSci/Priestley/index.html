Priestley’s Air Experiments Simulator

Explore how Joseph Priestley used candles, animals, plants and heated mercuric oxide to investigate different “airs”. Compare his historical interpretation with the modern oxygen explanation.

Learning Intention

Students will understand that Priestley’s experiments provided evidence that air contains substances involved in burning and breathing, even though his explanation was limited by the scientific ideas of his time.

Success Criteria

Students can describe Priestley’s observations, compare historical and modern explanations, and use simulation evidence to explain why scientific models change over time.

Simulation only: do not use mercury compounds, sealed animals or enclosed flames as classroom practicals.

Student challenge

  1. Run the plant-in-light sequence. Describe what happens before and after the plant is added.
  2. Run the plant-in-darkness sequence. Compare the candle test with the light trial.
  3. Switch to Priestley-style mode. Explain the result without using the words oxygen or carbon dioxide.
  4. Switch to modern mode. Explain the same result using oxygen, carbon dioxide, combustion and respiration.
  5. Use evidence from the data table to explain why scientific models can change over time.

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning

Teacher notes

  • This is a simplified classroom model. The gas percentages are not exact historical measurements.
  • The mouse is represented symbolically. Do not recreate sealed-animal experiments.
  • Mercuric oxide is hazardous and should not be used as a student practical.
  • A safer practical link could use aquatic plants, hydrogen carbonate indicator, limewater, oxygen sensors or carbon dioxide sensors.
  • Historical focus: Priestley gathered important evidence, but his phlogiston-based explanation was later replaced by oxygen-based chemistry.
  • Simulation assessment: students should identify what the model helps explain, its limitations, and how it differs from a real investigation.
