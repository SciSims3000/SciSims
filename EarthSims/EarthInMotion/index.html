Are seasons caused by Earth’s distance from the Sun?

Earth is closest in early January (perihelion, about 147.1 million km), during Australia’s summer, and farthest in early July (aphelion, about 152.1 million km). Both hemispheres share that distance, but experience opposite seasons. Axial tilt changes their sunlight angles and daylight duration in opposite ways and is the dominant cause of seasons.

Record both dates, then repeat at zero tilt. Distance still varies. Latitude still matters: zero tilt does not make all locations equally warm. This simulation does not calculate temperature.

Teacher guide

Learning intention: explain predictable Sun–Earth phenomena using relative positions and motion. This activity supports Year 7 Observing the Universe; it does not model the Moon, phases or eclipses.

Suggested sequence

Begin at Sydney, December, noon. Ask students to predict illumination at 18:00 and 00:00. At 18:00 Sydney is still in daylight in this model; at midnight it is dark. Follow the observer, then connect eastward rotation with apparent westward solar motion. Change longitude at one fixed Greenwich solar reference time to compare local solar times.

Compare June and December at Sydney, the equator and both poles. Require one controlled comparison and a causal explanation for each investigation. Repeat at zero tilt to separate tilt effects from distance. Familiar tilt-driven seasons largely disappear, while latitude differences and the small orbital-distance effect remain.

Expected explanations

Rotation is Earth’s daily eastward spin; revolution is its yearly orbit. Day and night occur as the observer rotates into and out of the illuminated hemisphere. The hemisphere tilted sunward generally receives longer days and a higher midday Sun, while the opposite hemisphere receives the reverse. At equinox the hemispheres are comparably illuminated. At the equator, daylight remains approximately 12 hours; the noon Sun’s annual height still changes.

Model limits

The model uses a Kepler ellipse with fixed eccentricity and approximate seasonal dates. It freezes the date during daily rotation. A fixed north axis is shown over one year; precession is omitted. Sunrise and sunset refer to the solar centre crossing a level geometric horizon; refraction and the Sun’s angular diameter are omitted. Published sunrise times will differ. Exact pole/zero-declination conditions put the solar centre on the horizon all day, so the ideal day length is undefined. Compass direction is undefined at exact poles and at the zenith/nadir. No temperature, terrain, weather or lunar model is included.

Space, orbit and noon cross-section views use different stated cameras. The cross-section observer is a latitude reference at noon. Local apparent solar time is not civil time. The Greenwich reference ignores the equation of time.

Assess prediction, fair comparison, evidence and causal explanation. Reveal conceptual answers after students have written their reasoning.
